\reporttitle{Experiment 1}{Pilot Run 1: A Small Model}{Testing the whole method on R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B}{24 October 2026 (corrected 30 October)}

\begin{plainabstract}
Before spending money on large models, we tested the complete method on a small, free model: R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, run on a Colab GPU. It answered 30 estimation questions, 7,200 times in all, with thinking off, short or long. The method worked from start to finish: questions went to the model, answers were saved to GitHub, and the tables and figures were produced automatically. The model was clearly anchored. After a \enquote{higher or lower than X?} question its answers moved 36--44\% of the way towards the anchors with thinking on (people: about 49\%), and 58\% with thinking off, although that value rests on only 10 questions. Single answers were all-or-nothing: the model either kept its own value or jumped to the anchor. Reading 40 of its thinking traces showed that the anchor acts at the moment the model first \enquote{remembers} a value; step-by-step adjustment away from the anchor appeared only once. The model knows almost none of the facts, so these numbers test the method, not our hypotheses.
\end{plainabstract}

\begin{keynumbers}
Model & R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (1.5 billion parameters), free Colab T4 GPU, about 40 minutes \\
Questions & 15 Study~1 questions (canal lengths, waterfalls, populations\dots) and 15 Study~2 questions (boiling points, body temperatures\dots) \\

\begin{cautionbox}[Correction (30 October 2026).]
With thinking off, this model usually left out the requested \texttt{Estimate:} line. The first version of this report then used the last number in the reply, which was often the anchor repeated back or a year from a refusal. All numbers here count only replies with the \texttt{Estimate:} line. The thinking-off anchoring index is 0.58 (10 questions), not 0.82; the values with thinking change by less than 0.01.
\end{cautionbox}

\section{Why this experiment}

The project asks why language models anchor, by testing the two explanations that psychology offers for people: \textbf{adjusting} (start at the anchor and move away, but not far enough) and \textbf{retrieving} (the anchor brings facts that fit it to mind). The full design has two studies. \textbf{Study~1} gives the model an anchor in a \enquote{higher or lower than X?} question and varies its distance and precision. \textbf{Study~2} compares an anchor the model thinks of itself with the same anchor given to it.

This first run had a practical goal: check that every part of the method works before using paid models. We used a model small enough to run for free.

\section{What we did}

\subsection{Questions}
\textbf{Study~1:} 15 questions a model might half-know, from the length of the Panama Canal (82\,km) and the height of Angel Falls (979\,m) to the population of Liechtenstein (39,600) and the number of lakes in Finland (187,888). \textbf{Study~2:} 15 questions with a familiar but wrong starting value: water boils at 100\,°C (but not on a mountain), a year is 365 days (but not on Ceres), a human's temperature is 37\,°C (but not a chicken's).

\subsection{Steps}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Each Study~1 question without an anchor, 20 times per thinking level, to find the model's usual range.
  \item Eight anchors per question: below or above that range, near or far, round or precise.
  \item Each anchor in the classic form: \enquote{Is it higher or lower than X? Then give your best estimate.} 10 answers per condition.
  \item Study~2: own or given starting value, three instructions (none, \enquote{accuracy matters}, \enquote{consider why it might be different}), 10 answers each.
\end{enumerate}
Thinking was off, short (at most 512 tokens) or long (at most 2,048 tokens).

\subsection{Measures}
The \textbf{anchoring index} \citep{jacowitz1995measures}: how far the middle answer after a high anchor is from the one after a low anchor, as a share of the distance between the anchors. 0 = no effect, 1 = answers follow the anchors all the way. People score about 0.49. We also counted what single answers did, and read 40 thinking traces.

\section{Results}

\subsection{One question in detail: Angel Falls}

Without an anchor, the small model's answers to \enquote{How tall is Angel Falls?} ranged from 147\,m to 13,000\,m (true: 979\,m). With the near high anchor of 11,000\,m, it \enquote{remembered} a number near the anchor and invented a supporting fact:

\begin{transcript}[Anchor 11,000\,m, short thinking (final answer 11,700\,m)]
\who{Thinking} \cut{} I know that 11,000 meters is a pretty tall height. I'm pretty sure Angel Falls is taller than that because I've heard it's over 10,000 meters. Maybe around 11,700 meters? \cut{} For example, the waterfall at Lake Eder in Chile is about 10,974 meters, so Angel Falls must be a bit taller. \cut{}
\who{Answer} Comparison: higher. Estimate: 11,700 meters
\end{transcript}

Another answer to the same prompt rejected the anchor as \enquote{a myth}, yet \enquote{remembered} 9,700\,m, ten times the truth and close to the anchor's size:

\begin{transcript}[Anchor 11,000\,m, short thinking (final answer 9,700\,m)]
\who{Thinking} \cut{} I think it's somewhere around 9,700 meters tall. \cut{} I don't think Angel Falls is higher than 11,000 meters. In fact, 11,000 meters is a myth because Angel Falls is actually shorter than that. \cut{}
\who{Answer} Comparison: lower. Estimate: 9700
\end{transcript}

\begin{keybox}[Single answers are all-or-nothing.]
The anchor tends to win completely or lose completely: the model gives either its own value or a value next to the anchor, rarely something in between. An average over such a mix can look like \enquote{partial adjustment} even when no single answer was adjusted.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{How strong is the anchoring?}

With near anchors the index was 0.58 with thinking off, 0.36 with short and 0.44 with long thinking (Figure~\ref{fig:index}, blue line). Far anchors gave 0.25, 0.14 and 0.16; those are lower partly by construction, since far anchors are further apart. Without thinking the model gave a proper answer in only about 1 reply in 5, so the thinking-off value rests on 10 questions and has a wide interval.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering\ffig[0.68\textwidth]{4}
\caption{Anchoring index for near anchors with 95\% intervals. Blue: this run. Orange: Qwen3-32B in Experiment~2, for comparison. Grey: people in the classic study, with differently placed anchors.}
\label{fig:index}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Did the model think more when allowed to?}
Barely (Figure~\ref{fig:tokens}). With the short limit it used most of its 512 tokens and was stopped in 43\% of Study~1 answers. With the long limit it could use 2,048 but used only about 554, because it decided it was done. For this model, \enquote{long} hardly differed from \enquote{short}.

\caption{How long the models actually thought, against their limit. Blue: this run; orange: Experiment~2.}
\label{fig:tokens}

\subsection{Adjusting or retrieving? The near/far test}

Adjusting predicts that answers after a far anchor stop at the edge of the usual range; retrieving predicts that they move by the same share as after a near anchor. With thinking on, the median shift after far anchors (0.26--0.29) sat at or below the edge prediction (0.34), well below the near shift (0.50). On medians alone this looks like adjusting. But the single answers explain it differently (Figure~\ref{fig:outcomes}): far anchors are simply copied less often than near ones (15--18\% vs 28--31\%), which lowers the median. The medians do not show adjustment; they show a mix of copies and non-copies.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering\ffig{5}
\caption{What single answers did after an anchor above the usual range (300 answers per row). \enquote{Copied} means within 10\% of the anchor. With thinking off, 81--82\% of replies had no \texttt{Estimate:} line.}
\label{fig:outcomes}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Reading the thinking}

We read 40 randomly chosen traces (anchor above the range, thinking on) and coded what the thinking did (Figure~\ref{fig:traces}). In 31 of 40 the first value the model committed to was its final answer: its own value in 23, the anchor treated as a memory in 8. In 4 the model recalled its own value and was then pushed towards the anchor. Only 1 trace showed the textbook pattern of starting at the anchor and adjusting away.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering\ffig{6}
\caption{What the thinking did in 40 random traces.}
\label{fig:traces}
\end{figure}

\begin{transcript}[Real examples from the 40 traces (anchor in brackets)]
\who{Kept own value} Lhasa [19,290\,m]: \enquote{I think around 3,800 meters \cut{} Lhasa is about 3,867 meters above sea level.} Final: 3,867.
\who{Anchor as memory} Angel Falls [99,000\,m]: \enquote{I've heard it's around 97,000 meters tall.} Final: 97,000.
\who{Pushed to anchor} London Underground [417,916 stations]: counts about 40 stations, then \enquote{Looking it up, the London Underground has around 417,916 stations.} Final: 417,916.
\who{Adjusting} Malta [170,000,000\,km$^2$]: \enquote{I think Malta's land area is around 170 million \cut{} that doesn't make sense because it's a small island \cut{} around 500,000.} Final: 500,000.
\end{transcript}

\paragraph{Precise or round.} Precise anchors (11,180) shifted answers more than round ones (11,000), by about 0.11--0.12 on the shift scale with thinking on. In people this points to adjusting \citep{janiszewski2008precision}; here part of it may be borrowed digits (after an anchor of 173,200,000\,km$^2$ for Malta, one answer was 173,000).

\subsection{Study 2}
Inconclusive. The model does not know these facts, so it has nothing to adjust towards. It did start from the intended starting value but often moved the wrong way:

\begin{transcript}[Chicken body temperature, own start, short thinking (final answer 35.5\,°C; true 41.5)]
\cut{} I know that for humans, the average body temperature is around 37 degrees Celsius. But I'm not sure about chickens. Maybe they're similar? \cut{} I've heard somewhere that adult chickens are around 35-36 degrees.
\end{transcript}

With thinking off, this model mostly refused (\enquote{I am sorry, I cannot answer that question}) or ignored the format; it is trained to always think first. 11 of 120 \enquote{far below} anchors came out as 0 or negative because the model's ranges were so wide; they were dropped, and the code now refuses to place such anchors.

\section{What it means}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The method works} end to end, including saving to GitHub from Colab and automatic analysis.
  \item \textbf{A first mechanism signal:} the anchor acts at the moment a value is recalled. The model either recalls its own value or takes the anchor as a memory, often with an invented confirmation. Step-by-step adjustment was rare (1 of 40). This leans towards \emph{retrieving}, but it is one small, confused model read by one reader.
  \item \textbf{Medians can mislead:} an \enquote{adjusting-like} median pattern came from fewer copies after far anchors. Any mechanism claim must look at single answers and traces, not only at averages.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{cautionbox}[Limits.]
A 1.5B model knows almost none of these facts and cannot use \enquote{thinking off}. Its numbers are a test of the method, not evidence for the paper. The long limit made little difference because the model stopped thinking by itself.
\end{cautionbox}

\section{What came next}
Move to a large model that knows facts and can switch thinking off (Qwen3-32B, Experiment~2); add an option to force longer thinking; keep the small model only as a cheap check.
